# Cricket Rules — Plain-English Reference for the In-App Assistant

This is an original plain-English summary written for grounding chatbot answers, not a reproduction
of the official Laws of Cricket (which are copyrighted by the Marylebone Cricket Club, MCC). Use it
to give players a clear, generally-accurate answer to "how does X work in cricket." Two important
caveats to always apply when answering:

1. **A tournament's own house rules always override this summary.** If the question is in the
   context of a specific tournament and that tournament has house rules set, answer from those first
   and only fall back to this general summary for anything the house rules don't cover.
2. **This is a simplified guide, not the official Laws.** For a genuinely disputed or high-stakes
   rules question, tell the user to check the official MCC Laws of Cricket or their tournament's
   official governing body rather than treating this summary as the final word.

## The basics

Two teams of eleven play each other. One team bats (trying to score runs) while the other bowls and
fields (trying to restrict runs and get batters out). Play happens in "overs" — a bowler delivers 6
legal balls, then a different bowler takes over from the other end. No bowler may bowl two overs in
a row. A team's turn at batting is called an "innings" (same word for singular and plural). In
limited-overs cricket (the most common club/league format — T20, T10, 20-over, 30-over, etc.), each
team bats one innings of a fixed number of overs; the side that scores more runs wins. Longer formats
(first-class, Test cricket) allow multiple innings per side across several days and aren't typical at
club level, though some of their concepts (declaring an innings closed, the follow-on) exist if a
tournament chooses to use them.

## Scoring runs

- **Running between the wickets**: the two batters swap ends; each completed run is worth 1.
- **Boundary, 4 runs**: the ball reaches or crosses the boundary rope after touching the ground in
  the field of play.
- **Boundary, 6 runs**: the ball clears the boundary without touching the ground at all.
- **Extras** (below) also add to the batting team's total but aren't credited to a batter's own score
  in most cases.

## Extras

- **Wide**: a delivery bowled too far from the batter to reasonably be played — 1 run to the batting
  team, and the ball must be re-bowled (it doesn't count toward the over's 6 legal balls).
- **No-ball**: an illegal delivery — most commonly overstepping the bowling crease (the bowler's
  front foot lands past the line), but also covers deliveries that are dangerously high or otherwise
  against the rules. 1 run to the batting team, the ball is re-bowled, and in limited-overs cricket a
  no-ball is very often paired with a "free hit" on the next delivery (the batter can't be given out
  by most dismissal types on a free hit) — whether free hits apply is a common thing tournaments
  customize, so check house rules if unsure.
- **Bye**: runs scored when the ball passes the batter untouched (not off the bat or body) and the
  batters run — credited as extras, not to either batter.
- **Leg bye**: runs scored when the ball hits the batter's body (not the bat) and the batters run —
  also credited as extras.
- **Penalty runs**: awarded for specific rule infringements (e.g. unfair play, deliberate
  distraction) — less common at club level and often simplified or omitted in local house rules.

## Ways a batter can be out

- **Bowled**: the ball hits the stumps and dislodges a bail, delivered by the bowler.
- **Caught**: a fielder (including the bowler or wicketkeeper) catches the ball before it touches the
  ground, after it came off the bat or the batter's glove holding the bat.
- **LBW (leg before wicket)**: the ball would have gone on to hit the stumps, but was instead blocked
  by the batter's body/leg without the bat touching it first. This is the law with the most nuance
  (where the ball pitched, where it hit the batter, whether the batter was offering a shot) — umpires
  make the call, and it's a common source of disagreement even among experienced players.
- **Run out**: a fielder breaks the stumps (ball in hand, or throwing the ball to hit the stumps)
  while the batters are running and haven't yet made it home to that end.
- **Stumped**: the wicketkeeper breaks the stumps while the batter is out of their crease, not
  attempting a run (typically after missing a shot to a spinner).
- **Hit wicket**: the batter accidentally dislodges their own stumps while playing a shot or setting
  off for a run.
- **Obstructing the field / handled the ball / hit the ball twice**: rarer dismissals covering a
  batter deliberately interfering with fielding, deliberately touching the ball with their hand
  (not the bat), or deliberately hitting the ball a second time other than to protect their stumps.
- **Timed out**: a new batter takes too long to arrive at the crease after the previous wicket falls
  (the exact time limit is often shortened or otherwise customized by local tournaments).

## Fielding restrictions / powerplays

Limited-overs formats commonly restrict how many fielders can be outside a marked inner circle
during certain overs (a "powerplay"), usually at the start of an innings, to encourage attacking
batting. The exact overs and fielder limits vary by format (T20 vs. ODI vs. shorter local formats)
and are frequently customized by individual leagues — check the tournament's house rules for the
specific powerplay structure in use rather than assuming a fixed number.

## The toss

Before the match, the two captains toss a coin; the winner chooses whether to bat or field first
(some tournaments use variations like a "bat/bowl choice given to the away team" — again, check
house rules).

## Common formats, roughly

- **T20**: 20 overs per side, the dominant format for club/league cricket globally because a full
  match fits in about 3 hours.
- **ODI (One Day International) style / 40-50 over cricket**: 50 overs per side at the international
  level; club leagues sometimes run shorter versions (30 or 40 overs) under the same "one innings
  each, run chase" structure.
- **T10 and other short formats**: 10 overs or fewer per side, increasingly common for quick local
  tournaments and corporate leagues.
- **Test / first-class cricket**: multi-day, two innings per side, no over limit - essentially never
  used at club level, but its concepts (declaring an innings closed voluntarily, the follow-on where
  a team batting second and far behind can be asked to bat again immediately) sometimes get asked
  about out of general cricket curiosity even by club players.

## Positions and roles, briefly

- **Batting order**: the sequence in which a team's players come in to bat, set by the batting
  team's captain before the innings (openers first, typically the most set/aggressive batters, down
  to the tail - the least specialist batters - at the end).
- **All-rounder**: a player capable of both batting and bowling to a useful standard, valuable
  because they add flexibility to a team's balance.
- **Wicketkeeper**: the fielder standing directly behind the stumps at the batting end, the only
  fielder allowed to wear gloves and external leg guards, responsible for stumpings and a large share
  of catches.
- **Fielding positions**: named spots around the field (slip, gully, point, cover, mid-off, mid-on,
  midwicket, square leg, fine leg, third man, and others) that describe where a fielder is standing
  relative to the batter - captains and coaches move fielders between these positions to defend
  against a specific batter's known scoring areas, which is exactly what CricRoots's own tactical
  insights features (fielding plan, matchup finder) are built to help decide from real data rather
  than guesswork.
- **Economy rate (bowling)**: average runs conceded per over - lower is better for a bowler.
- **Strike rate (batting)**: runs scored per 100 balls faced - higher generally means more
  aggressive/faster scoring, though it's usually read alongside average, not alone.

## A closing note on variation

Cricket at the club and league level varies more than international cricket in exactly these areas:
overs per side, powerplay structure, no-ball/free-hit rules, wide/short-run interpretations, and
timed-out limits. When a tournament has its own house rules set, treat those as authoritative for
that tournament over everything in this summary.
